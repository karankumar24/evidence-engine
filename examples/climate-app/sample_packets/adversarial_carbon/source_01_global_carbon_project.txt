Global Carbon Project — Global Carbon Budget 2022
Friedlingstein et al., Earth System Science Data (Synthetic Excerpt for Demo)

FOSSIL CO2 EMISSIONS

Global fossil CO2 emissions in 2022 are estimated at 36.6 GtCO2 (range: 36.2–37.0 GtCO2),
approaching the 2019 pre-pandemic record of 36.7 GtCO2. This represents a recovery from
the 5.4 percent decline observed during 2020 due to COVID-19 restrictions, followed by a
5.9 percent rebound in 2021. The 2022 value is 1 percent above the 2019 pre-pandemic peak.

LAND-USE CHANGE EMISSIONS

Emissions from land-use change (primarily tropical deforestation) averaged 3.9 GtCO2 per year
over 2012–2021. Preliminary estimates for 2022 place land-use emissions at approximately
3.9 GtCO2, consistent with the recent decade average. Total anthropogenic CO2 emissions
for 2022 are therefore estimated at 40.5 GtCO2 (fossil 36.6 + land-use 3.9).

GROWTH RATE

The decadal average growth rate of fossil CO2 emissions for 2012–2021 was 1.5 percent per
year, lower than the prior decade average of 2.9 percent per year (2002–2011). The slowdown
reflects a combination of improving energy efficiency, the rapid deployment of renewables in
major economies, and structural economic changes.

CARBON SINKS

The global carbon budget is balanced by uptake in land and ocean sinks. Over 2012–2021:
- Oceanic sink: 2.9 GtCO2 per year (range: 2.6–3.2)
- Land biosphere sink: 3.1 GtCO2 per year (range: 2.6–3.6)
- Combined sink: approximately 5.6 GtCO2 per year absorbed on average
- Atmospheric growth rate: approximately 5.2 GtCO2 per year (equivalent to ~2.5 ppm yr-1)

CONCENTRATION TRAJECTORY

The atmospheric CO2 concentration will continue to rise as long as global emissions remain
positive. The cumulative anthropogenic CO2 burden in the atmosphere at the start of 2023
is estimated at approximately 720 GtC above pre-industrial levels.

REMAINING BUDGET IMPLICATIONS

At 2022 fossil emission rates of 36.6 GtCO2 per year and total emissions of 40.5 GtCO2
per year, a remaining carbon budget of 500 GtCO2 (50% probability, 1.5°C) would be
exhausted in approximately 12 years from 2020 if emissions remain at current levels.
