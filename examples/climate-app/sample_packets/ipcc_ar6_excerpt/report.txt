Global Climate Assessment: Key Findings 2023

This report summarises the most significant findings from recent climate science assessments,
drawing on observational data and synthesis reports from the past two years. It is intended
as a reference for policymakers and researchers reviewing the current state of the climate system.

TEMPERATURE AND RADIATIVE FORCING

Global mean surface temperatures have increased by 1.1°C above pre-industrial levels since
the late 19th century [1]. The rate of warming observed over the period 2011–2020 is
approximately 0.19°C per decade, roughly double the rate recorded from 1979 to 2010 [1].
Human-induced radiative forcing is estimated at 2.72 W/m² for the year 2019 relative to
1750 [1]. Methane concentrations in 2022 reached 1923 parts per billion (ppb), the highest
level observed in ice-core records extending back 800,000 years [2].

ATMOSPHERIC CARBON DIOXIDE

Atmospheric CO2 concentrations reached 421 parts per million (ppm) in May 2023, the highest
level recorded in at least 800,000 years [1]. The current CO2 concentration is roughly
50 percent higher than pre-industrial levels of approximately 280 ppm [1]. The five-year
average growth rate of atmospheric CO2 for 2018–2022 was 2.5 ppm per year [2].

CRYOSPHERE OBSERVATIONS

Arctic sea ice extent in September 2022 was the tenth-lowest on record at 4.67 million km²,
continuing a long-term decline averaging 13 percent per decade since satellite observations
began [2]. The Greenland ice sheet lost an average of 280 Gt per year over the period
2010–2019, contributing approximately 0.77 mm per year to global mean sea level rise [1].
Antarctic ice sheet losses have tripled in the past decade compared with the previous ten-year
period [1].

SEA LEVEL AND OCEAN HEAT

Global mean sea level rose by 20 cm over the twentieth century, and the rate of rise has
accelerated to 3.7 mm per year for the period 2006–2018, up from 1.4 mm per year during
1901–1971 [1]. Ocean heat content has increased at all measured depths since the 1970s and
now accounts for roughly 91 percent of total excess energy absorbed by the Earth system [1].
The upper 2000 m of the ocean warmed by 0.13°C per decade from 2006 to 2020 [2].

EXTREME EVENTS

The probability of a global mean temperature anomaly exceeding 1.5°C above pre-industrial
levels for at least one year in the next five years is now estimated at 48 percent [1].
Heat extremes that occurred once every 50 years in the pre-industrial climate now occur
roughly once every decade under current warming of 1.1°C [1].
